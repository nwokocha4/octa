Sub-Category: Applied Mathematics

Task: Reconstruct a heterogeneous mobility field from sparse, noisy phase-separation imaging

A binary mixture is imaged while it phase-separates. The camera gives us a sparse, unevenly
covered snapshot sequence of the concentration field at four early times, with correlated
measurement noise. The mixture's mobility is not uniform -- the sample is a graded composite,
so mobility varies across it -- and the job is to recover that variation, say honestly which
parts of it the data actually support, and predict how the sample keeps separating at three
later times nobody got to observe.

Why is this task genuinely difficult?

The core challenge is telling mobility structure the observations support apart from structure
that merely fits noise. A good training fit can sit next to completely wrong transport
properties and completely wrong later predictions. That ambiguity is the whole task: the agent
has to notice it, handle it, and still produce a stable reconstruction that predicts forward
in time.

The type of professional who does this work in the real world is a computational materials
scientist or a chemical engineer working on phase-separating systems -- thin-film blends for
organic photovoltaics, polymer compatibilization, thin-film alloying, battery-electrode
microstructure, emulsions, or enhanced oil recovery with mobility control. Their day job is
exactly this loop: image the sample while it evolves (in-situ fluorescence microscopy,
X-ray or neutron phase-contrast imaging, time-resolved scattering), infer a spatially varying
transport coefficient from what they saw, and then simulate forward to predict behaviour the
experiment never reached. That inference step is what gets paid for. It is what turns a
simulation from descriptive into predictive, and it is the piece a modelling scientist has to
defend in a design review.

The data is synthetic, generated by a forward simulation the agent never sees. I built the
realism in rather than assuming it. The observation operator is a point-spread blur, because a
real imaging system has finite resolution and every pixel reports an average over its
neighbourhood, plus heteroscedastic measurement noise. The result is spatially correlated
error, and the covariance is handed to the agent as a factored matrix. Sensor coverage is
uneven on purpose: density varies across the field and one region sits in a poorly observed
shadow, so some directions of the mobility field are nearly invisible in places and well
constrained in others. Nine mobility coefficients, four observation times, and a few thousand
correlated sensor pixels is enough to fit but not to determine -- the noise pushes the
estimate along directions the data barely constrain, which is precisely the realistic
situation in imaging-based transport inference.

The pitfalls that produce wrong answers are characteristic, and each one is caught by a
different check, so nothing fails silently. Writing the flux as M times the Laplacian instead
of the divergence of M times the gradient looks harmless and destroys mass conservation; the
mass-drift gate catches it, and the energy-balance gate catches it harder, because the
free-energy dissipation law only holds in divergence form. Getting the free energy or the
gradient penalty kappa wrong changes the interface width and the coarsening rate, which shows
up in the withheld-time prediction. Treating the noise as white instead of using the supplied
correlation over-weights the well-covered region and misestimates everywhere else -- invisible
in the training misfit, visible only in prediction. Submitting a constant mobility is the
trivial null and it fails the predictive gate by design. An unconstrained parameterization can
go locally negative, which the admissibility bounds reject. Fitting the training times exactly
with no coherent spatial structure gives a field that looks fine early and is visibly wrong
later. And resolving everything on a fine grid while reporting meaningless coefficients is
closed off by a consistency check between the submitted grid and the submitted coefficients.
An agent that is merely competent will walk into at least one of these; telling them apart is
where the domain knowledge lives -- nonlinear inverse problems, conservative fourth-order PDE
modeling, and uncertainty propagation -- not in grinding through a big grid.

What is the intended solution approach?

Treat it as a regularized inverse problem and let the data say which directions to trust. A
competent practitioner would rebuild the observation operator from the supplied files, write
down the likelihood under the supplied covariance, add the supplied prior on the mobility
coefficients as a penalty, and run a gradient-based nonlinear least-squares or MAP
optimization, with finite-difference sensitivities or an adjoint formulation to keep each
iteration affordable. Then they would look at the answer the way an inverse-problem person
does -- which coefficients moved, which barely moved, where the curvature is smallest -- and
shrink accordingly. Finally they would simulate forward to the withheld times and check
against the withheld measurements.

The key insight is that the coefficients are not equally recoverable, and the poorly
constrained ones should be shrunk toward the prior rather than left at whatever maximizes the
training fit. The maximum-likelihood reconstruction is systematically wrong in exactly the
directions the data do not constrain, and worse so when the correlation is ignored, because
unweighted fitting over-counts redundant information. The prior-shrunk estimate looks worse
on training misfit and predicts better. Seeing that trade-off and acting on it is the task.
No particular algorithm or library is required -- Bayesian, Tikhonov, ensemble, MCMC, all
fine.

This shape of problem has been solved many times over: PDE-constrained coefficient estimation
with a Gaussian prior and likelihood is textbook Bayesian inverse-problem material, and the
forward model is a standard phase-field relaxation. Nothing here is unsolved research; the
composition is new but every piece is routine for the right person. A senior practitioner who
already knows how to set up this kind of problem should need about five to six focused hours:
inspecting the arrays and the covariance, building the inverse problem, getting scaling and
convergence right, and validating against the withheld-time prediction. The agent gets eight
hours, deliberately generous, so the budget measures reasoning rather than typing speed.

How will the solution be verified?

The agent works in /app and writes two files under /app/output: reconstruction.npz holding
mobility_coefficients (9,), mobility_grid (128, 128), and predicted_concentration
(3, 128, 128), plus diagnostics.json holding weighted_observation_misfit,
relative_mass_drift, and energy_balance_residual. Shapes, ordering, prediction times, and the
diagnostic formulas are all pinned down in the instruction; the pass/fail numbers are not
published, by design.

A separate verifier, running in its own container with no network, re-simulates the submitted
mobility with its own pinned solver and re-checks everything from scratch. It never compares
against the generating coefficients and never takes the agent's reported numbers on faith.
Passing means all of these hold at once: the output contract (files, shapes, dtypes, finite
values); the grid equals the basis expansion of the submitted coefficients; the mobility is
positive and inside the admissible range; the submitted prediction is a genuine simulation of
the submitted coefficients, conserves mass, satisfies the energy-balance bound, and its
reported diagnostics match independent recomputation; the verifier's own re-simulation hits
the withheld observations within a covariance-derived threshold (this is the primary
scientific gate); and the re-simulation conserves mass.

Because the truth is not uniquely recoverable, two reconstructions count as equivalent when
they are predictively equivalent -- their withheld-time predictions agree within observational
uncertainty. Exact agreement with the generating coefficients is neither required nor checked.
Thresholds are calibrated against baselines run per instance: constant mobility, the prior
mean, unweighted least squares, the unregularized maximum-likelihood fit, and the oracle
itself. The constant null must fail by a wide margin and the oracle must pass with margin;
a baseline that fails by a hair would be a calibration failure, not a shippable task.

Category and sub-category

Category: Science, Sub-category: Applied Mathematics. The deliverables are an admissible
coefficient reconstruction, a statement of which components the data support, and a predictive
validation against held-out measurements -- applied-mathematics deliverables for a fourth-order
conservation law, with identifiability analysis and uncertainty-aware regularization. I am
placing it here rather than in Materials Science on purpose: the competencies under test are
regularization, sensitivity analysis, and identifiability, and the physics, while load-bearing
(the divergence form is what makes the energy diagnostic meaningful), is supplied as context
rather than something the agent is asked to research.
