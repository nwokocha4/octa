# Reconstruct a heterogeneous mobility field from sparse, noisy phase-separation imaging

> **Draft v1.** Marked `[PILOT-n]` items are quantities that must be fixed by the pilot study
> described in §5.7 and `NOTES.md` before submission; they are placeholders, not measurements. Every
> other number here is a design decision, not an estimate. Review this against `REVIEW.md` (the v0
> evaluation) — this revision applies FIX 1–FIX 8.

## 0. Summary

A binary mixture is imaged while it phase-separates. The imaging returns a sparse, unevenly covered,
correlated-noise snapshot sequence of the concentration field at four early times. The mixture's mobility
is not uniform: it varies across the sample because the sample is a graded composite, and the task is to
recover that variation. The agent must reconstruct the mobility field, quantify how much of it the
observations actually constrain, and predict the phase separation at three withheld later times.

---

## 1. Category and sub-category

**Category:** Science **Sub-category:** Applied Mathematics

The deliverables are an admissible reconstruction of a spatially varying coefficient, a defensible
statement of which components of it the data supports, and a predictive validation against held-out
measurements. Those are applied-mathematics deliverables: ill-posed coefficient estimation for a
fourth-order conservation law, with identifiability analysis and uncertainty-aware regularization. The
physical system is the *substrate* that makes the inverse problem well-posed and interesting — it is
supplied as context, not something the agent is asked to research. The agent is never asked which
mobility is thermodynamically plausible, what the free energy should look like, or why phase separation
happens; it is given the forward model and asked to invert it.

We place this in Applied Mathematics rather than Materials Science deliberately. The competencies under
test are regularization, sensitivity analysis, and identifiability — a practitioner from a materials
background who knows phase-field theory thoroughly but not nonlinear inverse-problem methodology would
fail this task, while a computational mathematician who has never written a phase-field model but has
solved ill-posed coefficient problems would succeed. Conversely, the physics is not decorative: the
*divergence form* of the flux is essential to the free-energy dissipation law, and that law is what
makes the energy-balance diagnostic a meaningful check rather than a formality.

## 2. Why this task is genuinely difficult

### 2a. The professional and the value

A computational materials scientist or a chemical engineer working on phase-separating systems — thin-film
blends for organic photovoltaics, polymer blend compatibilization, thin-film alloying and dealloying,
corrosion-front and battery-electrode microstructure, emulsion and foam rheology, and enhanced oil
recovery with mobility control — routinely has to answer exactly this question: *what are the actual
transport properties of my material, and how confident am I in them?* The standard workflow is to image
the sample while it evolves (in-situ fluorescence microscopy, X-ray/neutron phase-contrast imaging, time-
resolved small-angle scattering), infer a spatially varying transport coefficient from the observed
evolution, and then use that coefficient in a simulation to predict behaviour the experiment could not
reach in its time window. That inference step is the deliverable, and it is paid for: it is what makes a
simulation predictive rather than merely descriptive, and it is what a modelling scientist is asked to
defend in a design review.

### 2b. The data

**Synthetic**, generated by a forward simulation the agent does not receive. The realism is engineered,
not incidental:

- **Correlated noise.** The observation operator is a point-spread blur — the imaging system has a
  finite resolution, so a pixel's reported concentration is an average over a neighbourhood — plus
  heteroscedastic measurement noise. The resulting error field is spatially correlated, and the
  covariance is *supplied to the agent* as a factorized matrix. Weighting observations as if the errors
  were independent is a natural and consequential mistake; the supplied covariance is what makes the
  difference visible.
- **Uneven sensor coverage.** The sensor mask is not a uniform subsample: coverage density varies across
  the field, and part of the field lies in a region the optics cannot reach well. Some directions of the
  mobility field are therefore nearly invisible in some parts of the domain and well constrained in
  others — spatially varying observability, not a uniform one.
- **A genuinely ill-posed problem.** Nine mobility coefficients, four observation times, and a few
  thousand correlated sensor pixels is enough to *fit* but not to *determine*: the noise pushes the
  estimate along directions the data barely constrain.

### 2c. Characteristic wrong answers, and the gate that catches each

This is the part of the task that carries the difficulty, and each failure below is caught by a
different gate, so no single error is silently absorbed.

| # | Wrong answer | Caught by |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Uses the non-conservative flux `M∇²μ` (or writes `∂c/∂t = M(x)∇²μ` for a spatially varying `M`) instead of `∇·(M∇μ)`. | **G6** (mass drift) — and, more sharply, **G7**: the free-energy dissipation law `dF/dt = −∫M|∇μ|²` holds *only* in the divergence form, so the non-conservative flux violates the energy balance at O(1), not at solver-tolerance size. |
| (ii) | Uses the wrong free energy `f(c)` — a symmetric double well against a single-well or a linearized one, or the wrong `κ`. | **G5** (prediction) and **G7**: wrong `κ` changes the interface width and therefore the coarsening rate, which shows up in the withheld-time prediction. |
| (iii) | Treats the observation noise as white — diagonal weights, or unweighted least squares — instead of using the supplied correlation structure. | **G5**. Correlated observations are redundantly weighted, so an unweighted fit chases the noise in the well-covered region and misestimates where coverage is sparse. The error is invisible in the training misfit and visible only in the prediction. |
| (iv) | Submits a constant mobility (`θ = 0`), i.e. the trivial null. | **G5**. This is the benchmark's primary discriminator and it is calibrated to fail (§5.5). |
| (v) | Uses an unconstrained or sign-defective parameterization, yielding locally negative or non-finite mobility. | **G3** (admissibility bounds). |
| (vi) | Fits the training times exactly and reports a reconstruction that is unstable — e.g. the interpolation of the mobility that passes through the observed trajectory but has no coherent spatial structure. | **G4** (contract/consistency) and **G5**: a physically incoherent reconstruction produces a visibly wrong field at the withheld times. |
| (vii) | Resolves the mobility on a fine grid rather than in the declared basis and reports the grid without a valid coefficient expansion. | **G2** (consistency between the submitted grid and the submitted coefficients under the declared parameterization). |

### 2d. Why this is not busywork, trivia, or a trick

The task has few steps and no volume: read a handful of supplied arrays, write a forward-model wrapper
(the solver is provided), set up a nonlinear least-squares or Bayesian inverse problem, and validate. The
difficulty is not the number of steps — it is that **the training data does not determine the answer**,
and the agent has to work out which parts of its own fit are real. That is a judgment call, not an
implementation grind. The task is not hard because it requires knowing one obscure fact, and it is not
adversarial: there is no trick, no misdirection, and no answer that is only findable by guessing the
reviewer's intent.

### 2e. Difficulty calibration

The difficulty dial is the number of mobility degrees of freedom, and it is set deliberately:

- **Nine coefficients** in the declared basis. Global or derivative-free search over a 9-dimensional
  space is computationally prohibitive inside the agent budget: a single forward evaluation costs
  `[PILOT-1]` seconds, and a competent global optimizer needs on the order of 10⁴–10⁵ evaluations, which
  is 10–100× the budget. Gradient-based inference with sensitivities or adjoints is the viable route,
  which is the intended solution and is not forced by fiat — it is forced by cost.
- **The information is genuinely degraded.** We report the ratio of the smallest to largest eigenvalue of
  the Gauss–Newton (Fisher) information matrix for `θ` at the true solution `[PILOT-2]`, and the
  posterior predictive spread at the withheld times relative to the observational noise
  `[PILOT-3]`. The design target is a conditioning ratio small enough that an unregularized maximum
  likelihood reconstruction is visibly worse at prediction than a prior-regularized one, and large enough
  that the prior-regularized reconstruction is still informative.
- **The nulls separate.** The margin between the constant-mobility baseline and the correct
  reconstruction on the gated prediction metric is `[PILOT-4]`, and the acceptance thresholds are set so
  the null fails by a wide margin while the correct answer passes with margin (§5.5).

### 2f. Expert effort

**Five to six hours** for a focused computational mathematician who already knows how to set up a
regularized nonlinear inverse problem with a supplied prior and a supplied forward model — covering
inspection of the supplied arrays and covariance, building the inverse problem, getting the scaling and
convergence right, and validating against the withheld-time prediction. This is the rubric's "someone
who knows exactly what to do" estimate, not the hours to derive the method.

The agent budget is **8 hours (28 800 s)**, which is generous relative to that estimate on purpose: the
agent must also explore the environment, work out which supplied file carries the covariance, and
iterate on its numerics. Setting the two equal would make the task a wall-clock race rather than a
reasoning problem.

## 3. Intended solution approach

The strategy is to treat this as a regularized inverse problem and to let the data tell you which
directions to trust.

A competent practitioner would: reconstruct the observation operator from the supplied files (sensor
mask, blur kernel, covariance factor); implement the likelihood under the supplied covariance; add the
supplied prior on the mobility coefficients as a penalty; and run a gradient-based nonlinear
least-squares or MAP optimization, using finite-difference sensitivities or an adjoint formulation to
keep the cost per iteration tractable. They would then *look at the profile of the solution* — which
coefficients moved, which barely moved, and in which directions the curvature is smallest — and use that
to decide how much to shrink. Finally they would test the reconstruction by simulating forward to the
withheld times and comparing against the withheld measurements.

The key insight, which is what separates a good solution from a brute-force one, is that **the
coefficients are not equally recoverable, and the way to handle the poorly-constrained ones is not to
ignore the data but to regularize toward the prior rather than toward an unconstrained optimum.** The
maximum-likelihood reconstruction of a spatial coefficient field from sparse, correlated, unevenly
covered observations is systematically wrong in exactly the directions the data do not constrain, and it
is systematically wrong *more* when the correlation is ignored — because unweighted fitting over-counts
the redundant information in the well-covered region. The prior-shrunk estimate is worse on the training
misfit and better on the prediction. Recognizing that trade-off and acting on it is the whole task.

The task does not require this method. Any route that produces a stable, physically admissible
reconstruction that predicts well is accepted — a Bayesian formulation, a Tikhonov-regularized
least-squares fit, or an ensemble/MCMC approach would all work. No particular algorithm, library, or
solver is required, and the instruction says so.

**Solvability argument.** This problem shape is standard and has been solved many times: nonlinear
ill-posed coefficient estimation for a PDE-constrained forward model, with a Gaussian prior and a
Gaussian likelihood, is the canonical Bayesian inverse problem (Stuart 2010; Stuart & Stedinger 1995),
and the spatial-coefficient case with a smoothness/GP prior is the canonical *degenerate* case
(the null space of the adjoint is where the difficulty lives). Applied to phase-field models, the
forward model is a textbook free-energy relaxation. Nothing here is an unsolved research problem; the
composition is new but each piece is routine for the right practitioner.

## 4. The environment the agent works in

- **Working directory:** `/app`.
- **Supplied inputs** (all read-only, all in `/app/data`):

  | File | Contents |
  |---|---|
  | `initial_condition.npz` | The concentration field `c₀` on the declared grid; the grid spacing and domain extent; the free-energy coefficients `f` (as coefficients) and `κ`; the mean composition |
  | `observations.npz` | The observed field snapshots at the four observation times, on the sensor mask; the mask itself; the blur kernel; the Cholesky factor `L` with `Σ_ε = L Lᵀ`; the noise variance model; the sensor positions |
  | `basis.npz` | The nine orthonormal basis functions `φ_k` on the grid, in the declared order, and the constant `M₀` |
  | `prior.json` | The prior mean and covariance on the coefficient vector `θ` |
  | `mobility_solver/` | A pinned, documented Python package implementing the forward model (§4.1) |

- **Pre-installed software:** NumPy, SciPy, and the pinned solver package; nothing else is needed, and
  the agent may install anything else it likes (the agent phase has open internet).
- **4.1 The forward model, as supplied.** The solver package implements, on the declared grid, with
  homogeneous no-flux boundary conditions:

  ```
  ∂c/∂t = ∇·( M(x) ∇μ ),      μ = f'(c) − κ ∇²c,      M(x) = M₀ · exp( Σ_k θ_k φ_k(x) )
  ```

  with `f(c) = ¼ c²(1 − c)²`, `κ` supplied, `c ∈ [0,1]`, `M₀` supplied. It exposes a single entry point
  that takes a coefficient vector and a list of times and returns the field at those times. Its
  discretization is documented and pinned.

  **We ship the solver deliberately.** The task is hard because the inverse problem is hard, not because
  the fourth-order PDE is annoying to implement. If the agent had to write the solver, the scientific
  content would drown under numerical-methods busywork and the benchmark would measure something else.
- **Compute:** `[PILOT-5]` CPU, `[PILOT-5]` GB RAM, no GPU. One forward evaluation to the prediction
  times costs `[PILOT-1]` seconds, so a gradient-based method with a few dozen iterations and a
  9-parameter finite-difference Jacobian is comfortably inside the budget, while a global search over
  the same space is not (§2e).
- **Network:** open for the agent phase (it may install packages). The verifier phase has none.

## 5. Outputs and deterministic verification

### 5a. The output contract

The agent must produce, in `/app/output`:

**`reconstruction.npz`**

| Array | Shape | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `mobility_coefficients` | `(9,)` float64 | `θ` in the declared basis order (`basis.npz`) |
| `mobility_grid` | `(128, 128)` float64 | `M(x)` evaluated on the declared grid |
| `predicted_concentration` | `(3, 128, 128)` float64 | The concentration field at the three prediction times, in the declared order |

**`diagnostics.json`**

| Key | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `weighted_observation_misfit` | The χ²-type misfit of the submitted reconstruction against the supplied observations, under the supplied covariance |
| `relative_mass_drift` | The relative change in total mass across the submitted prediction window |
| `energy_balance_residual` | The normalized discrepancy between the free-energy change of the submitted prediction and the dissipation integral |

The instruction states the exact definitions of all three diagnostics as formulas, states the
observation times and prediction times, states the sensor mask and blur operator, and states that
`mobility_grid` must equal `M₀ · exp(Σ_k θ_k φ_k)` evaluated on the declared grid under the supplied
basis. It does **not** state the pass/fail thresholds (see §5.4).

### 5b. The generative model

This is what makes the answer well-defined, and it is supplied in full:

- The true coefficients are `θ* ~ N(0, Σ_θ)`, drawn **once** by the generator from the supplied prior and
  written out to `authoring/provenance/theta_true.json` at generation time. `θ*` is never present in any
  agent-visible file.
- The true mobility is `M*(x) = M₀ exp(Σ_k θ*_k φ_k(x))`.
- The forward trajectory `c(t; θ*)` is generated by the reference solver.
- Observations are `y_j = (K_blur ⊛ c)(x_j) + ε_j` at the sensor positions `x_j`, where `ε ~ N(0, Σ_ε)`,
  `Σ_ε = L Lᵀ` with `L` supplied, and `Σ_ε` combines the heteroscedastic measurement-noise variance model
  with the spatial correlation induced by the blur.
- The agent is given: `c₀`, the grid, `f`, `κ`, `M₀`, the basis `{φ_k}`, the prior `Σ_θ`, the sensor
  mask, the blur kernel, the noise variance model, and `L`. The agent is **not** given `θ*`, the true
  trajectory, or the withheld observations.

The agent therefore knows the exact family the truth was drawn from. That is what makes the estimation
problem well-posed *and* what makes the "regularize rather than chase the likelihood" insight
available: the prior is part of the problem statement, not a guess the agent has to invent.

### 5c. The gates

PASS requires **all** of:

- **G1 · Output contract.** `reconstruction.npz` and `diagnostics.json` exist; every array is present with
  the declared shape, dtype, and ordering; all values are finite.
- **G2 · Coefficient/grid consistency.** `mobility_grid` equals `M₀ · exp(Σ_k θ_k φ_k)` evaluated on the
  declared grid from the supplied basis, to a tight relative tolerance. (Closes the loophole of
  submitting a good grid with meaningless coefficients, or the reverse.)
- **G3 · Admissibility.** `min(mobility_grid) > 0`, and the mobility field lies within the declared
  admissible range `[PILOT-6] · M₀` to `[PILOT-6] · M₀`.
- **G4 · Physical consistency of the submitted prediction.** The agent's `predicted_concentration`
  satisfies the mass-conservation and free-energy-monotonicity conditions to the stated tolerances, and
  the agent's three reported diagnostics agree with the verifier's independent recomputation from the
  submitted fields to the stated tolerances.
- **G5 · Predictive accuracy — the primary scientific gate.** The verifier re-simulates the submitted
  `mobility_coefficients` with its own pinned solver, applies the same observation operator (blur and
  sensor mask), and computes the covariance-weighted misfit against the **withheld observations at the
  three prediction times**, which were generated from an independent noise draw and never seen by the
  agent. The weighted misfit must fall below a threshold derived from the supplied covariance at a stated
  confidence level.
- **G6 · Conservation.** The relative mass drift of the verifier's re-simulation must fall below the
  stated tolerance, and the energy-balance residual must fall below its stated tolerance.

G5 is what the task is about; G6 is what catches the wrong flux form; G4 catches a submission that
reports numbers it did not actually compute; G1–G3 make the rest meaningful.

**The grade comes from the verifier's own simulation of the submitted reconstruction, not from the
agent's submitted field or reported numbers.** The agent's `predicted_concentration` is checked for
contract and physical consistency (G4) and for agreement with the verifier's independent re-simulation
to within a stated solver tolerance that brackets independently established discretization errors —
but the scientific gate is G5, computed from `mobility_coefficients` alone. This is deliberate: it means
the task grades the *inference*, not the agent's numerics, so an agent whose solver differs from the
reference within the stated tolerance is not penalized for that.

### 5d. What "multiple valid answers" means operationally

The truth is not uniquely recoverable, and the task does not pretend otherwise. Two reconstructions are
accepted as equivalent when they are **predictively equivalent**: their predictions at the withheld
times, pushed through the observation operator, agree within the observational uncertainty. The verifier
implements this by grading both reconstructions against the same withheld observations under G5 — it
never compares a submitted reconstruction to `θ*`. Exact agreement with the generating coefficients is
not required and is not checked.

### 5e. Calibration and the rejection baselines

Thresholds are not published in the instruction. They are set by the pilot study against these
baselines, each of which is run against each gated instance separately:

| Baseline | Expected outcome | Why it is the right null |
|---|---|---|
| Constant mobility (`θ = 0`) | **fails G5** | The trivial null; the simplest admissible reconstruction |
| Prior mean (`θ = 0`, same as above under a zero-mean prior) | fails G5 | The estimator a lazy or over-regularized agent produces |
| Unweighted least squares (diagonal weights, no covariance) | **fails G5**, passes G4 | The characteristic error (iii): fits the training data, predicts badly |
| Maximum likelihood, no prior | **fails G5** | The characteristic error: overfits along the weakly-constrained directions |
| Oracle (true `θ*`, and the MAP reconstruction) | **passes all gates** with margin | Establishes solvability |
| The agent's own `predicted_concentration` re-simulated by the verifier | agrees within the solver tolerance | Confirms the agent's field is a genuine simulation of its stated reconstruction |

Acceptance criteria for the calibration itself: the constant-mobility baseline must fail G5 by at least
`[PILOT-7]`× the gate threshold, and the oracle must pass G5 with at least `[PILOT-8]`× margin. A
baseline that fails by a hair is a calibration failure, not a passing task.

### 5f. What the agent cannot reach

- **The withheld observations and the true coefficients** live only in `tests/`, baked into the verifier
  image, and are never copied into the agent environment or into the agent image layers.
- **The generator script** that produced the data lives in `authoring/provenance/`, which Harbor never
  mounts into a container.
- **The verifier has no network egress**, so it cannot reach a hosted answer key, and the submitted
  artifacts cannot phone home.
- **The reported diagnostics are not the graded quantity.** G4 requires them to be *consistent* with the
  verifier's recomputation; it does not take the agent's word for them.

## 6. Anchoring and distinctness

**Anchoring.** The forward model is the standard phase-field (Cahn–Hilliard) relaxation of a binary
mixture with a regularized double-well free energy, in the hydrodynamic-theory tradition where the
transport coefficient enters as a spatially varying Onsager mobility. The inference problem is the
canonical Bayesian inverse problem for a partially observed, nonlinear, spatially varying parameter
(stuart 2010; Stuart & Stedinger 1995), and its difficulty is the classical one: the adjoint's null
space is where the data are silent, and a prior is what makes a stable reconstruction possible. The
observational model — a point-spread-limited, heteroscedastic, correlated-noise image of a phase-separating
sample — is what in-situ fluorescence or phase-contrast imaging of a blend actually delivers, including
its uneven coverage.

**Distinctness from merged tasks.** The closest tasks in the benchmark are `rbc-temperature-inference`
(forward-model parameter inference in Rayleigh–Bénard convection), `traffic-flux-inversion`,
`koopman-mfg-id`, `ode-law-discovery`, `certified-sparse-regression`, and `localized-sspd-solver`. This
task differs on four axes at once: the unknown is a **spatially heterogeneous field**, not a lumped
parameter or a sparse coefficient vector; the evidence is **multi-time**, with the discriminating
evidence held out at *later* times rather than fitted; the explicit difficulty is **degenerate
directions and identifiability**, not sparsity; and the physical constraint that does the verifying is a
**conservation law with a free-energy dissipation identity**, not a generic regularizer. It also differs
from `rbc-temperature-inference` in that the quantity inverted is a field with a positivity constraint,
the noise is spatially correlated rather than assumed independent, and the predictive gate is a
withheld-time measurement rather than a diagnostic.

## 8. Questions a reviewer will ask

1. **How do you know this is identifiable enough to be solvable?** Because the prior is supplied and the
   problem is a standard Bayesian inverse problem; the pilot reports the conditioning of the information
   matrix at the truth (`[PILOT-2]`) and the posterior predictive spread (`[PILOT-3]`).
2. **What happens if the agent just optimizes the training misfit?** It gets a reconstruction that passes
   G4 and fails G5 — that is the calibrated maximum-likelihood baseline (§5e).
3. **Does the threshold actually reject the trivial answers?** Yes, and the calibration *requires* a
   minimum separation (`[PILOT-7]`), so a marginal separation is treated as a pilot failure rather than
   shipped.
4. **Is this a real workflow or a course project?** A real workflow — inferring spatially varying
   transport properties from in-situ imaging of phase separation (§2a, §6).
5. **Could a generalist solve it with generic skills?** Generic curve-fitting would produce the
   maximum-likelihood baseline, which fails the predictive gate. Passing requires reasoning about
   regularization and identifiability.
6. **Why Applied Mathematics and not Materials Science?** §1 argues the tested competencies, not the
   substrate.
7. **How long does one forward evaluation take, and can the method finish inside the budget?**
   `[PILOT-1]`, `[PILOT-5]`. This is the one number that most constrains the task and it is a pilot
   output.
8. **Which criterion are you weakest on?** Difficult — because the calibration is asserted rather than
   measured until the pilot runs.
