Reconstruct a heterogeneous mobility field from sparse, noisy phase-separation imaging

A binary mixture is imaged while it phase-separates. The imaging returns a sparse, unevenly covered,
correlated-noise snapshot sequence of the concentration field at four early times. The mixture's mobility
is not uniform: it varies across the sample because the sample is a graded composite, and the task is to
recover that variation. The agent must reconstruct the mobility field, quantify how much of it the
observations actually constrain, and predict the phase separation at three withheld later times.

CATEGORY AND SUB-CATEGORY

Category: Science
Sub-Category: Applied Mathematics

The deliverables are an admissible reconstruction of a spatially varying coefficient, a defensible
statement of which components of it the data supports, and a predictive validation against held-out
measurements. Those are applied-mathematics deliverables: ill-posed coefficient estimation for a
fourth-order conservation law, with identifiability analysis and uncertainty-aware regularization. The
physical system is the substrate that makes the inverse problem well-posed and interesting - it is
supplied as context, not something the agent is asked to research. The agent is never asked which
mobility is thermodynamically plausible, what the free energy should look like, or why phase separation
happens; it is given the forward model and asked to invert it.

We place this in Applied Mathematics rather than Materials Science deliberately. The competencies under
test are regularization, sensitivity analysis, and identifiability - a practitioner from a materials
background who knows phase-field theory thoroughly but not nonlinear inverse-problem methodology would
fail this task, while a computational mathematician who has never written a phase-field model but has
solved ill-posed coefficient problems would succeed. Conversely, the physics is not decorative: the
divergence form of the flux is essential to the free-energy dissipation law, and that law is what makes
the energy-balance diagnostic a meaningful check rather than a formality.

WHY THIS TASK IS GENUINELY DIFFICULT

The professional and the value.

A computational materials scientist or a chemical engineer working on phase-separating systems - thin-film
blends for organic photovoltaics, polymer blend compatibilization, thin-film alloying and dealloying,
corrosion-front and battery-electrode microstructure, emulsion and foam rheology, and enhanced oil
recovery with mobility control - routinely has to answer exactly this question: what are the actual
transport properties of my material, and how confident am I in them? The standard workflow is to image the
sample while it evolves (in-situ fluorescence microscopy, X-ray/neutron phase-contrast imaging, time-
resolved small-angle scattering), infer a spatially varying transport coefficient from the observed
evolution, and then use that coefficient in a simulation to predict behaviour the experiment could not
reach in its time window. That inference step is the deliverable, and it is paid for: it is what makes a
simulation predictive rather than merely descriptive, and it is what a modelling scientist is asked to
defend in a design review.

The data.

Synthetic, generated by a forward simulation the agent does not receive. The realism is engineered, not
incidental.

Correlated noise. The observation operator is a point-spread blur - the imaging system has a finite
resolution, so a pixel's reported concentration is an average over a neighbourhood - plus heteroscedastic
measurement noise. The resulting error field is spatially correlated, and the covariance is supplied to
the agent as a factorized matrix. Weighting observations as if the errors were independent is a natural
and consequential mistake; the supplied covariance is what makes the difference visible.

Uneven sensor coverage. The sensor mask is not a uniform subsample: coverage density varies across the
field, and part of the field lies in a region the optics cannot reach well. Some directions of the
mobility field are therefore nearly invisible in some parts of the domain and well constrained in others
- spatially varying observability, not a uniform one.

A genuinely ill-posed problem. Nine mobility coefficients, four observation times, and a few thousand
correlated sensor pixels is enough to fit but not to determine: the noise pushes the estimate along
directions the data barely constrain.

Characteristic wrong answers, and the gate that catches each.

This is the part of the task that carries the difficulty, and each failure below is caught by a different
gate, so no single error is silently absorbed.

(i) Uses the non-conservative flux M grad^2 mu (or writes dc/dt = M(x) grad^2 mu for a spatially varying
M) instead of div(M grad mu). Caught by the mass-drift gate, and more sharply by the energy-balance gate:
the free-energy dissipation law dF/dt = -integral M |grad mu|^2 holds only in the divergence form, so the
non-conservative flux violates the energy balance at O(1), not at solver-tolerance size.

(ii) Uses the wrong free energy f(c) - a symmetric double well against a single-well or a linearized one,
or the wrong kappa. Caught by the prediction gate and the energy-balance gate: wrong kappa changes the
interface width and therefore the coarsening rate, which shows up in the withheld-time prediction.

(iii) Treats the observation noise as white - diagonal weights, or unweighted least squares - instead of
using the supplied correlation structure. Caught by the prediction gate. Correlated observations are
redundantly weighted, so an unweighted fit chases the noise in the well-covered region and misestimates
where coverage is sparse. The error is invisible in the training misfit and visible only in the
prediction.

(iv) Submits a constant mobility (theta = 0), i.e. the trivial null. Caught by the prediction gate. This
is the benchmark's primary discriminator and it is calibrated to fail.

(v) Uses an unconstrained or sign-defective parameterization, yielding locally negative or non-finite
mobility. Caught by the admissibility-bounds gate.

(vi) Fits the training times exactly and reports a reconstruction that is unstable - e.g. the
interpolation of the mobility that passes through the observed trajectory but has no coherent spatial
structure. Caught by the consistency gate and the prediction gate: a physically incoherent reconstruction
produces a visibly wrong field at the withheld times.

(vii) Resolves the mobility on a fine grid rather than in the declared basis and reports the grid without
a valid coefficient expansion. Caught by the consistency gate between the submitted grid and the submitted
coefficients under the declared parameterization.

Why this is not busywork, trivia, or a trick.

The task has few steps and no volume: read a handful of supplied arrays, write a forward-model wrapper
(the solver is provided), set up a nonlinear least-squares or Bayesian inverse problem, and validate. The
difficulty is not the number of steps - it is that the training data does not determine the answer, and
the agent has to work out which parts of its own fit are real. That is a judgment call, not an
implementation grind. The task is not hard because it requires knowing one obscure fact, and it is not
adversarial: there is no trick, no misdirection, and no answer that is only findable by guessing the
reviewer's intent.

Difficulty calibration.

The difficulty dial is the number of mobility degrees of freedom, and it is set deliberately.

Nine coefficients in the declared basis. Global or derivative-free search over a 9-dimensional space is
computationally prohibitive inside the agent budget: a single forward evaluation costs [PILOT-1] seconds,
and a competent global optimizer needs on the order of 10^4-10^5 evaluations, which is 10-100x the budget.
Gradient-based inference with sensitivities or adjoints is the viable route, which is the intended solution
and is not forced by fiat - it is forced by cost.

The information is genuinely degraded. We report the ratio of the smallest to largest eigenvalue of the
Gauss-Newton (Fisher) information matrix for theta at the true solution [PILOT-2], and the posterior
predictive spread at the withheld times relative to the observational noise [PILOT-3]. The design target
is a conditioning ratio small enough that an unregularized maximum likelihood reconstruction is visibly
worse at prediction than a prior-regularized one, and large enough that the prior-regularized
reconstruction is still informative.

The nulls separate. The margin between the constant-mobility baseline and the correct reconstruction on
the gated prediction metric is [PILOT-4], and the acceptance thresholds are set so the null fails by a
wide margin while the correct answer passes with margin.

Expert effort.

Five to six hours for a focused computational mathematician who already knows how to set up a regularized
nonlinear inverse problem with a supplied prior and a supplied forward model - covering inspection of the
supplied arrays and covariance, building the inverse problem, getting the scaling and convergence right,
and validating against the withheld-time prediction. This is the rubric's "someone who knows exactly what
to do" estimate, not the hours to derive the method.

The agent budget is 8 hours (28800 s), which is generous relative to that estimate on purpose: the agent
must also explore the environment, work out which supplied file carries the covariance, and iterate on its
numerics. Setting the two equal would make the task a wall-clock race rather than a reasoning problem.

INTENDED SOLUTION APPROACH

The strategy is to treat this as a regularized inverse problem and to let the data tell you which
directions to trust.

A competent practitioner would: reconstruct the observation operator from the supplied files (sensor mask,
blur kernel, covariance factor); implement the likelihood under the supplied covariance; add the supplied
prior on the mobility coefficients as a penalty; and run a gradient-based nonlinear least-squares or MAP
optimization, using finite-difference sensitivities or an adjoint formulation to keep the cost per
iteration tractable. They would then look at the profile of the solution - which coefficients moved, which
barely moved, and in which directions the curvature is smallest - and use that to decide how much to
shrink. Finally they would test the reconstruction by simulating forward to the withheld times and
comparing against the withheld measurements.

The key insight, which is what separates a good solution from a brute-force one, is that the coefficients
are not equally recoverable, and the way to handle the poorly-constrained ones is not to ignore the data
but to regularize toward the prior rather than toward an unconstrained optimum. The maximum-likelihood
reconstruction of a spatial coefficient field from sparse, correlated, unevenly covered observations is
systematically wrong in exactly the directions the data do not constrain, and it is systematically wrong
more when the correlation is ignored - because unweighted fitting over-counts the redundant information in
the well-covered region. The prior-shrunk estimate is worse on the training misfit and better on the
prediction. Recognizing that trade-off and acting on it is the whole task.

The task does not require this method. Any route that produces a stable, physically admissible
reconstruction that predicts well is accepted - a Bayesian formulation, a Tikhonov-regularized
least-squares fit, or an ensemble/MCMC approach would all work. No particular algorithm, library, or
solver is required, and the instruction says so.

Solvability argument. This problem shape is standard and has been solved many times: nonlinear ill-posed
coefficient estimation for a PDE-constrained forward model, with a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian
likelihood, is the canonical Bayesian inverse problem (Stuart 2010; Stuart & Stedinger 1995), and the
spatial-coefficient case with a smoothness/GP prior is the canonical degenerate case (the null space of
the adjoint is where the difficulty lives). Applied to phase-field models, the forward model is a
textbook free-energy relaxation. Nothing here is an unsolved research problem; the composition is new but
each piece is routine for the right practitioner.

THE ENVIRONMENT THE AGENT WORKS IN

Working directory: /app.

Supplied inputs (all read-only, all in /app/data):

  initial_condition.npz - the concentration field c0 on the declared grid; the grid spacing and domain
  extent; the free-energy coefficients f and kappa; the mean composition.

  observations.npz - the observed field snapshots at the four observation times, on the sensor mask; the
  mask itself; the blur kernel; the Cholesky factor L with Sigma_eps = L L^T; the noise variance model;
  the sensor positions.

  basis.npz - the nine orthonormal basis functions phi_k on the grid, in the declared order, and the
  constant M0.

  prior.json - the prior mean and covariance on the coefficient vector theta.

  mobility_solver/ - a pinned, documented Python package implementing the forward model.

Pre-installed software: NumPy, SciPy, and the pinned solver package; nothing else is needed, and the
agent may install anything else it likes (the agent phase has open internet).

The forward model, as supplied. The solver package implements, on the declared grid, with homogeneous
no-flux boundary conditions:

  dc/dt = div( M(x) grad mu ),   mu = f'(c) - kappa grad^2 c,   M(x) = M0 * exp( sum_k theta_k phi_k(x) )

with f(c) = (1/4) c^2 (1-c)^2, kappa supplied, c in [0,1], M0 supplied. It exposes a single entry point
that takes a coefficient vector and a list of times and returns the field at those times. Its
discretization is documented and pinned.

We ship the solver deliberately. The task is hard because the inverse problem is hard, not because the
fourth-order PDE is annoying to implement. If the agent had to write the solver, the scientific content
would drown under numerical-methods busywork and the benchmark would measure something else.

Compute: [PILOT-5] CPU, [PILOT-5] GB RAM, no GPU. One forward evaluation to the prediction times costs
[PILOT-1] seconds, so a gradient-based method with a few dozen iterations and a 9-parameter
finite-difference Jacobian is comfortably inside the budget, while a global search over the same space is
not.

Network: open for the agent phase (it may install packages). The verifier phase has none.

OUTPUTS AND DETERMINISTIC VERIFICATION

The output contract.

The agent must produce, in /app/output:

  reconstruction.npz
    mobility_coefficients   (9,) float64     - theta in the declared basis order (basis.npz)
    mobility_grid           (128, 128) float64 - M(x) evaluated on the declared grid
    predicted_concentration (3, 128, 128) float64 - the concentration field at the three prediction
                                                          times, in the declared order

  diagnostics.json
    weighted_observation_misfit - the chi-square-type misfit of the submitted reconstruction against the
                                   supplied observations, under the supplied covariance
    relative_mass_drift       - the relative change in total mass across the submitted prediction window
    energy_balance_residual   - the normalized discrepancy between the free-energy change of the submitted
                                 prediction and the dissipation integral

The instruction states the exact definitions of all three diagnostics as formulas, states the observation
times and prediction times, states the sensor mask and blur operator, and states that mobility_grid must
equal M0 * exp(sum_k theta_k phi_k) evaluated on the declared grid under the supplied basis. It does not
state the pass/fail thresholds.

The generative model.

This is what makes the answer well-defined, and it is supplied in full.

The true coefficients are theta* ~ N(0, Sigma_theta), drawn once by the generator from the supplied prior
and written out to authoring/provenance/theta_true.json at generation time. theta* is never present in any
agent-visible file.

The true mobility is M*(x) = M0 exp(sum_k theta*_k phi_k(x)).

The forward trajectory c(t; theta*) is generated by the reference solver.

Observations are y_j = (K_blur convolved with c)(x_j) + eps_j at the sensor positions x_j, where eps ~
N(0, Sigma_eps), Sigma_eps = L L^T with L supplied, and Sigma_eps combines the heteroscedastic
measurement-noise variance model with the spatial correlation induced by the blur.

The agent is given: c0, the grid, f, kappa, M0, the basis {phi_k}, the prior Sigma_theta, the sensor mask,
the blur kernel, the noise variance model, and L. The agent is not given theta*, the true trajectory, or
the withheld observations.

The agent therefore knows the exact family the truth was drawn from. That is what makes the estimation
problem well-posed and what makes the "regularize rather than chase the likelihood" insight available:
the prior is part of the problem statement, not a guess the agent has to invent.

The gates.

PASS requires all of:

Output contract. reconstruction.npz and diagnostics.json exist; every array is present with the declared
shape, dtype, and ordering; all values are finite.

Coefficient/grid consistency. mobility_grid equals M0 * exp(sum_k theta_k phi_k) evaluated on the
declared grid from the supplied basis, to a tight relative tolerance. Closes the loophole of submitting a
good grid with meaningless coefficients, or the reverse.

Admissibility. min(mobility_grid) > 0, and the mobility field lies within the declared admissible range
[PILOT-6] M0 to [PILOT-6] M0.

Physical consistency of the submitted prediction. The agent's predicted_concentration satisfies the
mass-conservation and free-energy-monotonicity conditions to the stated tolerances, and the agent's three
reported diagnostics agree with the verifier's independent recomputation from the submitted fields to the
stated tolerances.

Predictive accuracy - the primary scientific gate. The verifier re-simulates the submitted
mobility_coefficients with its own pinned solver, applies the same observation operator (blur and sensor
mask), and computes the covariance-weighted misfit against the withheld observations at the three
prediction times, which were generated from an independent noise draw and never seen by the agent. The
weighted misfit must fall below a threshold derived from the supplied covariance at a stated confidence
level.

Conservation. The relative mass drift of the verifier's re-simulation must fall below the stated
tolerance, and the energy-balance residual must fall below its stated tolerance.

The predictive gate is what the task is about; the conservation gate is what catches the wrong flux form;
the physical-consistency gate catches a submission that reports numbers it did not actually compute; the
contract, consistency, and admissibility gates make the rest meaningful.

The grade comes from the verifier's own simulation of the submitted reconstruction, not from the agent's
submitted field or reported numbers. The agent's predicted_concentration is checked for contract and
physical consistency and for agreement with the verifier's independent re-simulation to within a stated
solver tolerance that brackets independently established discretization errors - but the scientific gate
is computed from mobility_coefficients alone. This is deliberate: it means the task grades the
inference, not the agent's numerics, so an agent whose solver differs from the reference within the stated
tolerance is not penalized for that.

What "multiple valid answers" means operationally.

The truth is not uniquely recoverable, and the task does not pretend otherwise. Two reconstructions are
accepted as equivalent when they are predictively equivalent: their predictions at the withheld times,
pushed through the observation operator, agree within the observational uncertainty. The verifier
implements this by grading both reconstructions against the same withheld observations - it never compares
a submitted reconstruction to theta*. Exact agreement with the generating coefficients is not required and
is not checked.

Calibration and the rejection baselines.

Thresholds are not published in the instruction. They are set by the pilot study against these baselines,
each of which is run against each gated instance separately.

Constant mobility (theta = 0) - fails the predictive gate. The trivial null; the simplest admissible
reconstruction.

Prior mean (theta = 0, same as above under a zero-mean prior) - fails the predictive gate. The estimator
a lazy or over-regularized agent produces.

Unweighted least squares (diagonal weights, no covariance) - fails the predictive gate, passes physical
consistency. The characteristic error: fits the training data, predicts badly.

Maximum likelihood, no prior - fails the predictive gate. The characteristic error: overfits along the
weakly-constrained directions.

Oracle (true theta*, and the MAP reconstruction) - passes all gates with margin. Establishes solvability.

The agent's own predicted_concentration re-simulated by the verifier - agrees within the solver
tolerance. Confirms the agent's field is a genuine simulation of its stated reconstruction.

Acceptance criteria for the calibration itself: the constant-mobility baseline must fail the predictive
gate by at least [PILOT-7]x the gate threshold, and the oracle must pass with at least [PILOT-8]x
margin. A baseline that fails by a hair is a calibration failure, not a passing task.

What the agent cannot reach.

The withheld observations and the true coefficients live only in tests/, baked into the verifier image, and
are never copied into the agent environment or into the agent image layers.

The generator script that produced the data lives in authoring/provenance/, which Harbor never mounts
into a container.

The verifier has no network egress, so it cannot reach a hosted answer key, and the submitted artifacts
cannot phone home.

The reported diagnostics are not the graded quantity. They are required to be consistent with the
verifier's recomputation; the verifier does not take the agent's word for them.

ANCHORING AND DISTINCTNESS

Anchoring. The forward model is the standard phase-field (Cahn-Hilliard) relaxation of a binary mixture
with a regularized double-well free energy, in the hydrodynamic-theory tradition where the transport
coefficient enters as a spatially varying Onsager mobility. The inference problem is the canonical
Bayesian inverse problem for a partially observed, nonlinear, spatially varying parameter (Stuart 2010;
Stuart & Stedinger 1995), and its difficulty is the classical one: the adjoint's null space is where the
data are silent, and a prior is what makes a stable reconstruction possible. The observational model - a
point-spread-limited, heteroscedastic, correlated-noise image of a phase-separating sample - is what
in-situ fluorescence or phase-contrast imaging of a blend actually delivers, including its uneven coverage.

Distinctness from merged tasks. The closest tasks in the benchmark are rbc-temperature-inference
(forward-model parameter inference in Rayleigh-Benard convection), traffic-flux-inversion, koopman-mfg-id,
ode-law-discovery, certified-sparse-regression, and localized-sspd-solver. This task differs on four axes
at once: the unknown is a spatially heterogeneous field, not a lumped parameter or a sparse coefficient
vector; the evidence is multi-time, with the discriminating evidence held out at later times rather than
fitted; the explicit difficulty is degenerate directions and identifiability, not sparsity; and the
physical constraint that does the verifying is a conservation law with a free-energy dissipation identity,
not a generic regularizer. It also differs from rbc-temperature-inference in that the quantity inverted
is a field with a positivity constraint, the noise is spatially correlated rather than assumed
independent, and the predictive gate is a withheld-time measurement rather than a diagnostic.
